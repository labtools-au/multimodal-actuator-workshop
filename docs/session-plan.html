TLDR;

This is how you spend two hours finding out what actuators actually feel like, and leave able to drive one from your own code.

  1. ⚡ Get a board and run task 01. Something spins.
  2. 🔁 Change two numbers, upload again. That loop is the whole workshop.
  3. 🖐️ Pick up the actuators you are not using. They are on the benches around you.
  4. 🔌 Work through as many of the five tasks as you get to. Nobody finishes all five.
  5. ✅ Build one signal someone else can read without looking.

Why this session exists

Premise

Every year, projects arrive at the pitch built entirely on the screen and the speaker, because those are the two output devices a computer scientist already owns. Touch gets designed on paper and then quietly dropped. Not because it was the wrong choice, but because nobody knew that a coin motor costs 12 kr and takes two wires.

So this is deliberately not a tutorial. There is no circuit to copy correctly. There are six benches, each with an actuator already wired and already running, and your job is to touch all of them and form an opinion about what each one is good at.

A coin motor and a solenoid can both signal “message received.” They are not interchangeable. One is a nudge and one is a knock, and that difference is a design decision, not a component decision.

You leave with a shopping list you actually believe in, and a rough sense of what the lab can lend you for the project weeks.

What this needs you to know first

  • noneNothing. This runs early on purpose, so no bench assumes a lecture you have already had.
  • afterThe vocabulary arrives later: actuator types, auditory feedback, and how to design feedback across several senses at once.
  • todayWhat you get here is the physical reference those lectures will point back to. Much easier to follow a slide about vibration when your wrist remembers it.

Run sheet

120 minutes

Times are offsets from the start. The rotation is the spine of the session, so keep it moving. Groups will want to linger at bench 3 and it will wreck the schedule.

0:0010 min

Two motors, one message

Buzz a phone-style notification on a coin motor, then send the same message on a solenoid. Ask which felt more urgent. Nobody needs theory to answer that, which is the point of the whole session.

0:1015 min

Boards out, everyone runs task 01

The only hard checkpoint. Task 01 is short on purpose: it flushes out the wrong board, the wrong port and the missing driver before anyone gets invested.

0:2560 min

Work in pairs. Pick up the actuators you are not driving. Ask for the ones you want to try.

1:2510 min

One sentence per group, no slides. This is where somebody reports that the Peltier takes eight seconds, which reliably kills two bad project ideas before they get proposed.

The five tasks

Upload, tweak, upload

Every sketch runs the moment you upload it. Nothing to wire. Each one has a CHANGE ME block at the top with two or three numbers, and a THINGS TO TRY list at the bottom with four experiments.

01

Make something spin

A coin motor you control. Find the speed where it gives up and stalls.

02

Make something tap

A solenoid. One tap, two, three. Same force, completely different message.

03

Make something warm

A Peltier tile. Time how many seconds before you would bet money on warmer.

04

Make it notice you

Touch input from a wire and a piece of foil. No sensor, no breakout board.

05

Make it answer back

Input drives output. The smallest complete interaction, and the shape of most project work.

Freestyle is encouraged. You do not have to do the tasks in order, and you do not have to finish them. Getting task 01 working and then properly playing with task 02 beats rushing through all five. If you want to drive something that is not on your bench, go and get it.
Before you power anything: the solenoid and the Peltier tile both pull real current and need their own supply. Ask before you rewire either. The Peltier needs its heatsink on, every time.

Inputs worth knowing about

Ten-minute mention

Not a rotation. These sit on one table with a sign, for the projects that need to sense a person rather than signal to one. Mention them at the framing and let people wander over during the build.

Heart rate / PPG

MAX30102 · I²C

Optical pulse from a fingertip. Honest warning: it is stable at rest and garbage the moment the hand moves, so design around a seated user.

GSR / skin conductance

Grove GSR · A1

Two finger electrodes reading arousal. Drifts constantly, so it is usable as a relative change over seconds, never as an absolute number.

Flex / bend

2.2" flex · A2 + 10k

A resistive strip that changes value as it bends. The cheap route to a data glove, and the obvious pairing with force feedback on bench 4.

Capacitive touch

ADCTouch · any analogue pin

The cheapest input on the table: a wire behind foil, cardboard or fabric, no breakout board. Costs one library and a rolling baseline. See bench 4; it is genuinely this easy.

Pressure / force

FSR · analogue + divider

A resistive pad that reads how hard, not just whether. Needs a two-point calibration per pad. They vary enough that one constant across six pads will not do.

Your own phone

already in your pocket

Accelerometer, gyro, mic, camera, and a vibration motor. For a lot of projects this beats anything on the table, and needs no soldering at all.

If it needs to be wireless

ESP32 · Wi-Fi + BLE

Swap the Uno for an ESP32 and most of the sketch survives, but you gain networking, which is the route to anything worn or anything logging to a server. Worth the extra setup only when the prototype genuinely cannot have a cable.

Two rigs that already exist

Prior art · show, don't slide

Both of these were built here, by students at roughly your stage, and both are worth five minutes of screen time during the framing. Not as things to copy, but as proof that the gap between "we should use touch" and "it moves when you touch it" is smaller than it looks.

Earlier student project · Arduino Uno

Capacitive sensing + servos + linear actuators

A moving screen that breathes when idle and retreats when you touch it. Worth showing because of what it does between the obvious states: an anticipation move before the big sequence, sine-eased servo travel rather than write() and hope, and an idle breathing loop that makes the object feel alive when nothing is happening.

It also ships a browser control panel over the serial port, so you can drive the rig from a laptop during a demo, which is exactly what you will wish you had at the project demonstrations.

The transferable bit: easing and idle motion are what separate "a servo moved" from "it reacted to me." Both are a dozen lines.
Bachelor project · ESP32

Instrumented sock, pressure logging

Six force-sensitive resistors under a foot (arch, heel, big toe and three edges), sampled on an ESP32 and batched over Wi-Fi to a PocketBase collection ten readings at a time. Each sensor carries its own two-point calibration constants, because supposedly identical FSRs were not.

Show it when someone proposes a wearable. It is a real answer to "how do I get sensor data off the body and somewhere I can analyse it", and the batching detail is the part people get wrong first.

The transferable bit: per-sensor calibration, and batching writes instead of one HTTP request per sample.

Closing brief

30 minutes

Encode three messages in one actuator

  1. Pick one actuator from the rotation. Any of the six.
  2. Choose three messages a real interface would need to send. Suggested set: message arrived, something is wrong, task finished.
  3. Encode each one by varying two things only: the rhythm of the pulses, and how strong they are. No changing actuators between messages.
  4. Hand the device to another group with the display turned away. They receive all three in a random order and name them.
  5. Write down the confusion: which two got mixed up, and what parameter would have separated them.

Constraint that makes it interesting: the recipient cannot look at the device. If your design only works when someone is watching a screen, you built a visual interface with a motor glued to it.

// Bench 1: signal skeleton. Rhythm is yours to change. // Strength = PWM depth. Rhythm = the on/off pattern below. const int MOTOR = 9; void pulse(int strength, int ms) { analogWrite(MOTOR, strength); // 0-255, strength delay(ms); analogWrite(MOTOR, 0); } void messageArrived() { pulse(160, 60); } // one soft tap void somethingWrong() { for (int i=0;i<3;i++){ pulse(255,90); delay(70);} } void taskFinished() { pulse(120,40); delay(60); pulse(220,220); } void setup() { pinMode(MOTOR, OUTPUT); } void loop() { /* trigger from the pot or a button */ }

Test it

Before you call it done

Hand your device to another group with the screen turned away.

  • They can tell all three messages apart.
  • The device does not fire continuously when held.
  • You know which two got confused, and what would have separated them.
  • It still works when they are not looking at it.

What you actually learn

Why it is worth two hours

Five small tasks give you three things you cannot get from a datasheet.

⚡ What things feel like

A motor is a nudge. A solenoid is a knock. A Peltier is slow. You now know that in your hands rather than in theory.

🔧 How to drive one

PWM, direction, debouncing, and why the motor never goes straight to a pin. Enough to add an actuator to a project without starting from zero.

🎯 What to pick

Choosing the actuator before designing the interaction, rather than discovering in week 46 that the one you picked cannot do it.

The important bit is not that your signal is elegant, or that you finish all five tasks. It is that you leave having actually driven something, and with an opinion about what it was good for.
